Our company and overall strategy

Module Robotics is a five-person South Florida startup selling modular robotic products to U.S. consumers. Its interoperable modules include drive, joint, gripper, sensor/compute, and power components. Our group chooses Path A: design in South Florida, manufacture and assemble in Mexico, then import to the United States. For the initial offering, Mexican workers assemble and test a complete starter robot from compatible modules, so a school can use it quickly while retaining the ability to reconfigure or replace modules later.

Production locationMexico only
Primary customersU.S. school robotics teams
Risk strategyU.S. backup capability, not an active second factory

Key distinction: Stop 1 commits actual module production to Mexico. Stop 7 keeps a contingency option for U.S. production if a defined risk threshold is reached; it does not mean we are currently manufacturing in both places.

Why our eight decisions fit together

Stop 1 — Build in Mexico

Mexico is our sole manufacturing location because our small team cannot efficiently operate two facilities. We accept border risk in exchange for potentially lower production cost. Our South Florida headquarters retains product design, customer support, and supplier oversight.

Stop 2 — Use a shelter provider

Our five founders do not have an internal Mexican legal, HR, and customs department. A shelter arrangement can help us begin compliant manufacturing without owning a Mexican legal entity at launch. We will still obtain professional advice on which entity is the exporter, importer of record, and employer, and whether any IMMEX arrangements apply.

Stop 3 — Import finished robots

We choose a finished starter robot assembled from interchangeable modules, not a sealed single-purpose machine. This gives school teams a ready-to-run platform while preserving modular design. The trade-off is packaging and transportation cost; we will measure whether shipping separate modules would be cheaper. Customs classification and USMCA eligibility must be determined from the actual product and bill of materials, not assumed from assembly in Mexico.

Stop 4 — Combine people and automation

Trained workers assemble mechanical and electrical parts; low-cost fixtures, torque tools, and automatic tests handle repetitive quality checks. This avoids investing immediately in an expensive fully automated Florida cell while supporting consistent safety and performance.

Stop 5 — Truck to a U.S. warehouse

We use scheduled cross-border truck shipments to replenish one U.S. fulfillment location. That warehouse can dispatch smaller orders to schools nationwide without requiring each customer shipment to clear the border. The warehouse creates inventory costs, so we start with conservative stock levels and track on-time delivery.

Stop 6 — Sell direct

Our website will offer classroom-ready starter robots and replacement modules directly to U.S. school teams. Direct selling gives us feedback about reliability, pricing, and the modules students actually use. The cost is that our small company must handle support and marketing itself.

Stop 7 — Hedge without opening a second factory

We will keep production in Mexico while documenting a U.S. assembly plan, identifying a potential contract manufacturer, and retaining digital work instructions and test specifications. We do not pay to run a second production line unless the switch signal fires. This is a staged contingency, not a claim that we currently operate two sites.

Stop 8 — Start with school robotics teams

Schools are the best initial fit for a modular system that can be rebuilt for new challenges. Teams value reliable delivery before competitions, replacement parts, understandable instructions, and predictable prices. This customer choice strengthens our case for the U.S. warehouse and suggests we should keep individual replacement modules available even though the initial robot ships assembled.

Shock round — Stop 7

Shock card drawn in class: Not provided / not confirmed.

Scenario used for planning: A hypothetical new tariff increases the cost of importing finished robots assembled in Mexico.

Did our switch signal fire? Conditional. Yes if the tariff raises fully landed cost by at least 10% versus the $160 baseline (to $176 or more); no if the increase is smaller. In the illustrative 10%-of-customs-value example, the cost rises to $174, so the cost-based signal does not fire.

We decided to: Hedge (prepare both options), while continuing Mexican production unless the actual cost or delay threshold is reached.

What does that cost us? For classroom planning, assume a one-time $4,000 qualification and testing budget for a U.S. backup assembler, plus $500 per month to maintain backup readiness. These are invented planning assumptions, not supplier quotations. We accept these costs to preserve flexibility rather than committing to a second operating factory.

Looking back

Did we switch paths at any stop? Which one, and why?

No actual path switch is part of our selected plan. We chose Mexico at Stop 1 and kept Mexican manufacturing through Stop 8. At Stop 7, we added a U.S. backup option, which is a hedge rather than a move to domestic production. We would only activate it after our measurable tariff, cost, or border-delay triggers are met.

After choosing our first customer (Stop 8), which earlier choice would we change?

We would refine Stop 3. School competition teams often need to replace individual parts or reconfigure a robot between challenges. We would still import a complete, tested starter robot as our initial product, but we would also stock separately replaceable modules in the U.S. warehouse. If customer feedback shows that most teams prefer building from individual modules, we would reconsider shipping modules rather than fully assembled robots. This retains our chosen initial strategy while responding to the first customer's needs.

What did we learn?

Nearshoring is not just a wage comparison. Mexico's possible assembly savings must be weighed against legal setup, customs compliance, freight, inventory, quality, and political risk. Innovation appears in the interchangeable robot design, the mixed human/automation assembly process, and the flexible supplier structure. Our strategy makes a clear bet on Mexico while keeping an affordable way to respond if conditions change.
